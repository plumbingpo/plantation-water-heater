Most tankless problems announce themselves the same way, with a blinking code on the display and a shower that turns cold. Behind that code is a system with very little margin for error. There is no storage tank to fall back on, so the heater has to detect flow, light a burner or power its elements, and raise the water temperature by fifty degrees or more in the few seconds it takes water to cross the heat exchanger. When scale narrows the passages, when the flow sensor stops reading, when the flame rod cannot confirm a flame, or when the vent or gas line cannot keep up, the unit protects itself by shutting down. That is the pattern we see week after week in Plantation, FL.

Error codes are a starting point, not a verdict, and we treat them that way on every Plantation call. The same ignition failure code can come from a worn igniter, a fouled flame rod, a gas line too small to feed the burner at full fire, or an air intake clogged with lint. That is why Plantation Water Heater tests before replacing anything. Our technicians read the fault history stored in the control board, watch flow sensor output with a faucet open, measure flame signal and igniter resistance, and put a manometer on the gas inlet to see what pressure actually reaches the valve when the burner is running flat out. We also compare inlet and outlet temperatures against the flow rate, which reveals a scaled heat exchanger long before it fails outright.

When to Call for Tankless Water Heater Repair

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • The display showing an error code instead of a temperature
  • Outlet temperature that surges and sags while water is running
  • Leaks under the unit or around its water connections
  • No hot water at a low-flow faucet even though the shower works fine
  • A cold-water sandwich that has gotten noticeably worse
  • Hot water that cannot keep up with more than one fixture
  • The unit clicking or trying to light several times before giving up
  • New whistling, roaring, or rumbling from the unit

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Start with what is at stake. A gas-fired tankless heater can burn nearly 200,000 BTU per hour at full fire, and its vent is the only thing carrying carbon monoxide out of the house; a joint that pulls loose or an intake that gets blocked changes that in a hurry. Electric tankless models are serious electrical loads, frequently more than 100 amps spread over several 240-volt circuits. Then there is the diagnostic puzzle. Error codes point to a system, not a part, so a flow sensor fault, a scaled heat exchanger, and a bad thermistor can look nearly identical on the display, and guessing gets expensive.

What you get from a professional is certainty. Instead of swapping parts, our technicians confirm the fault with a meter, a manometer, and temperature readings, so the fix is aimed at the actual cause. Scale is removed with a pump through the service valves rather than vinegar poured in and left to chance. Gas pressure is checked with the burner at full output, vent runs are inspected against the manufacturer's length and clearance limits, and the unit is tested at low and high flow before we leave. Proper service also keeps you in line with the manufacturer's warranty terms, many of which expect qualified service and regular descaling.

Prevention Tips That Actually Help

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Here are our recommendations:

  • Get the supply line checked before adding gas appliances, which can starve a tankless unit
  • Write down any error code before resetting the unit, and call if it keeps coming back
  • Keep the unit powered during freezing weather so its freeze protection can work
  • Schedule a descaling flush every year; hard water may call for one every six months
  • If scale comes back quickly, add a softener or a scale-prevention cartridge on the cold inlet
  • Clean the inlet water filter screen a few times a year so the flow sensor gets full flow
  • Make sure nothing blocks the exhaust vent or the air intake outside
